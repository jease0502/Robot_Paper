Where the Heat Actually Is

Decomposing actuator load in learned legged locomotion,
and the limits of command filtering

Author pending

Affiliation pending · Corresponding author pending

Preprint, draft v2 10 September 2026 Simulation only Unitree Go1, MuJoCo, policy frozen

Abstract—Reinforcement-learning locomotion policies frequently overheat commercial quadrupedal and humanoid actuators in operating regimes where vendor controllers remain thermally stable. The standard explanation attributes this dissipation to high-frequency command jitter and prescribes smoothness penalties in the reward function as the default remedy. That hypothesis has not been tested against the alternative: that learned policies adopt postures demanding higher steady-state torque. We separate the two mechanisms by showing that electrical copper loss decomposes exactly into a posture term and a ripple term which add in quadrature, so that their relative dominance is fixed by a joint's steady-state torque rather than by the policy. We locate this crossover analytically and empirically at 3.2 N·m on a Unitree Go1. Load-bearing calf joints sit above it and carry only 14–17% of their heat in bands a filter can reach; hip-abduction joints sit below it and carry 33–45%.

Evaluating post-hoc command filtering on frozen policies, a Parseval decomposition of joint torque shows that zero-order-hold staircase harmonics account for 1.6% of total heat, while 5–25 Hz command content carries the remaining discretionary dissipation. Open-loop replay suggests a 46% thermal reduction is available through zero-phase filtering, but that ceiling collapses in closed loop. Buying zero phase with 40 ms of latency degrades feedback stability, producing falls in 20% of episodes at 1.0 m/s on a well-regularized policy and in every episode on an unregularized one. A causal order-8 predictive filter with joint-load gating instead recovers 47% and 54% of the reachable thermal budget on the two checkpoints without inducing falls. Finally, we show that the widely deployed action_rate penalty does not govern command-side thermal load, whereas effort and energy penalties set the reachable budget.

Index terms—legged locomotion, reinforcement learning, actuator thermal load, copper loss, command smoothing, zero-order hold, spectral decomposition, closed-loop stability.

1.Introduction

Legged locomotion policies trained by reinforcement learning routinely reach actuator thermal limits in deployment. On a Unitree G1 humanoid running a whole-body controller, ankle-roll actuators reach roughly 90 °C within five to ten minutes of static standing, while the manufacturer's own controller holds the identical joints between 30 and 45 °C.[1] On a Unitree A1 carrying a 3 kg payload, baseline policies trip thermal protection in five to seven minutes.[2], [3] The relation is not one-directional: on a Go2-W a learned policy plateaus at 60 °C over 51 minutes of walking while the vendor controller diverges to 84 °C and shuts down at 34 minutes.[4] Policy behaviour clearly governs dissipation. Which behaviour is a matter of open disagreement.

Two hypotheses are in circulation. Under the posture hypothesis, dissipation is set by the steady holding torque the chosen kinematic posture demands; the Go2-W trials support this, attributing a 24 °C difference at 50 Hz to periodic foot lifting that relieved steady joint torque.[4] Under the jitter hypothesis, the excess comes from high-frequency command oscillation that inflates RMS current without producing useful torque; the G1 ankle-roll failure supports this, since static holding torque there is fixed by gravity and yet two controllers differ by 45–60 K.[1]

Neither hypothesis has been tested by measuring both terms independently. Copper loss requires that posture torque and dynamic torque ripple add in quadrature:

\begin{equation} \mathcal{H} \;\propto\; \mathbb{E}[\tau^2] \;=\; \underbrace{\bar{\tau}^2}_{\text{posture}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\tau)}_{\text{ripple}} . \end{equation}

A zero-mean command ripple produces no net torque and contributes its full variance to dissipation. Because the two terms add in quadrature, a large steady torque \(\bar{\tau}\) suppresses the relative contribution of \(\mathrm{Var}(\tau)\). Whether command jitter governs thermal load is therefore a property of joint-specific loading, not an intrinsic property of the policy. Both camps can be, and we argue are, describing different joints.

This paper locates the crossover between the two regimes, quantifies how much thermal recovery command filtering can deliver on a frozen policy, and identifies the closed-loop stability boundary that governs whether such a filter can be deployed at all.

Contributions

  1. Spectral heat decomposition. An exact Parseval-based split of joint torque into steady posture, gait-synchronous torque, in-band jitter (5–25 Hz), and zero-order-hold discretization harmonics (> 25 Hz). The reachable budget is 18.1% for a well-regularized Go1 policy and 43.3% for a policy trained without effort penalties, with ZOH harmonics accounting for 1.6%.
  2. Thermal load crossover. Out-of-band heating follows \(\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_0 + \kappa\rho/(1-\rho)\) to within 0.45% residual. Posture overtakes ripple at \(\bar{\tau} \approx \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\tau)} \approx 3.2\) N·m on a Go1. Joints below this threshold carry two to three times the filter-reachable heat share of joints above it.
  3. Closed-loop latency penalty. The 46% open-loop ceiling collapses to an 8.5% closed-loop reduction (Cliff's \(\delta = -0.33\), a small effect). Buying zero phase with 40 ms of latency compromises feedback margins: 20% fall rate at 1.0 m/s on the regularized policy, 100% on the unregularized one.
  4. Predictive gated filtering. An order-8 causal linear autoregressive filter recovers 47% and 54% of the filter-reachable budget on the regularized and unregularized policies respectively, with no falls. Gating by joint load preserves tracking fidelity.
  5. Reward ablation. Ablating action_rate raises step transients by 8%; ablating effort and energy penalties raises them by 89%. The penalty the field reaches for is not the one that governs command-side thermal load.

2.Actuation Pipeline and Problem Formulation

Commercial quadruped and humanoid architectures run proportional-derivative loops on the motor drive rather than inside the policy process. The policy emits position targets at \(f_{\text{pol}}\); these cross a communication bus to motor-side PD loops running at higher rate. On Unitree systems the policy executes at 50 Hz and commands are written at 500 Hz; DeepRobotics Lite3 deployments run policies near 83 Hz over a 1 kHz state loop. Actuator-level control reaches 6 kHz on the Unitree GO-M8010-6 and 1 kHz on the DeepRobotics J60.[6]

A zero-order hold (ZOH) sits between policy and low-level loop. At 50 Hz into 500 Hz each command is held for ten ticks and then steps discontinuously, producing an instantaneous proportional transient of \(k_p \Delta q\) before the rotor has moved.

In standardized environments such as unitree_rl_mjlab, PD gains are derived from a target closed-loop natural frequency of 10 Hz and a damping ratio \(\zeta = 2.0\), with action scaling set to \(0.25\,e/k_p\) where \(e\) is the effort limit. An action reversal between successive policy steps therefore commands a peak transient

\begin{equation} \tau_{\text{step}} \;=\; k_p \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{0.25\,e}{k_p} \;=\; 0.5\,e . \end{equation}

A full-scale action reversal commands a transient equal to half the joint torque limit. With \(\zeta = 2.0\) the dominant closed-loop pole sits at 2.68 Hz, while the 50 Hz command stream carries content up to its 25 Hz Nyquist limit. The gap between those two numbers is the subject of this paper.

3.Metrics

3.1Spectral heat decomposition

Applying Parseval's theorem to joint torque partitioned into mean and zero-mean components gives

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}[\tau^2] \;=\; \bar\tau^2 \;+\; \int_{0}^{f_g} S_\tau(f)\,\mathrm{d}f \;+\; \int_{f_g}^{f_{\text{Nyq}}} S_\tau(f)\,\mathrm{d}f \;+\; \mathbb{E}_k\!\left[\mathrm{Var}_{\text{sub}}(\tau)\right] , \end{equation}

where \(f_g = 5\) Hz isolates the fundamental gait frequencies, \(f_{\text{Nyq}} = 25\) Hz is the policy Nyquist frequency, and \(\mathbb{E}_k[\mathrm{Var}_{\text{sub}}(\tau)]\) is the intra-step variance above Nyquist arising from ZOH discretization. The last two terms together define the reachable share: the only part of the heat a command-stream filter can act on at all.

3.2Ripple fraction and out-of-band command power

When a power spectral density is unavailable we use the joint-level ripple fraction

\begin{equation} r \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\tau)}{\bar{\tau}^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\tau)} . \end{equation}

Command-side jitter is quantified independently of the plant by the out-of-band command power fraction

\begin{equation} \rho \;=\; \frac{\int_{f_c}^{f_{\text{Nyq}}} S_q(f)\,\mathrm{d}f}{\int_{0}^{f_{\text{Nyq}}} S_q(f)\,\mathrm{d}f} , \end{equation}

where \(f_c\) is the joint's \(-3\) dB closed-loop bandwidth, 15.3 Hz for the Go1 model of Section 4. Table 2 reports a second, coarser cut of the same quantity taken at 5 Hz; it is written \(\rho_{>5\,\mathrm{Hz}}\) throughout and is not interchangeable with \(\rho\).

3.3Thermal proxy

Motor thermal load is quantified as \(\mathcal{H} = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \tau^2\,\mathrm{d}t\), proportional to \(I_{\text{rms}}^2 R\) under a constant torque coefficient. Stator thermal dynamics justify squared rather than absolute torque: on calibrated brushless actuators the winding thermal time constant is 3.12 s against 777 s for the whole motor housing, a factor of 249.[7] Reported driver temperatures lag winding heat, which makes a current-based proxy the relevant primary metric.[8]

4.Experimental Setup

Four protocols are used. The labels are those of the source draft; there is no E4, as the label was never assigned.

E1 — single joint, synthetic commands. A second-order joint modelled on the Go1 (\(k_p = 35\), damping 0.5, armature 0.005 kg·m², \(f_n = 13.3\) Hz, \(\zeta = 0.60\), \(-3\) dB bandwidth 15.3 Hz) is driven by a 2 Hz sinusoidal command with bandpass noise injected above bandwidth. Commands are sampled at 50 Hz, held by ZOH to 500 Hz, and integrated at 5 kHz. Constant load torques are swept from 0 to 20 N·m.

E2 — recorded commands, open loop. Trajectories recorded from seven Go1 policies spanning a reward ablation are replayed through the single-joint model under different filtering schemes. Preserved motion is benchmarked against the ZOH trajectory low-passed at 5 Hz.

E3 — closed loop, full quadruped. A frozen Go1 policy is deployed in MuJoCo at 50 Hz policy rate, 500 Hz physics, ten substeps per policy step, with the filter inside the loop. Ten episodes of 500 steps are run at each of three commanded velocities (0.5 m/s, 1.0 m/s, and 0.3 m/s with turning) under matched random seeds and matched observation noise, giving \(n = 30\) per scheme.

E5 — spectral decomposition. The same closed-loop setup under baseline ZOH only, over 30 rollouts across the three velocity commands. Mean torque per policy step is logged at 50 Hz, with spectral splits at 5 Hz and 25 Hz and intra-step variance supplying the > 25 Hz component.

Predictive filter. An order-8 linear autoregressive model, AR(8), is fitted by least squares to baseline rollouts and rolled forward two steps, so that a symmetric Hann window can be applied without waiting for future samples and therefore without latency.

Load gate. Joints whose mean absolute torque \(|\bar{\tau}|\) falls below the 3.2 N·m crossover are filtered; the rest pass through unmodified.

At \(\rho = 0.2\) an unloaded joint dissipates 10.9 times the heat while moving 1.8% more; a joint carrying 20 N·m dissipates 2% more heat for the same command. In the unloaded condition dissipation follows \(\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_0 + \kappa\rho/(1-\rho)\) with \(\kappa = 38.4\) and a maximum residual of 0.45%. The crossover, where \(\bar{\tau}^2 = \mathrm{Var}(\tau)\), lies at 3.2 N·m.

Measured mean \(|\tau|\) per joint family on the Go1 is 1.24–1.48 N·m for hip abduction, 2.00–2.59 N·m for the thighs, and 5.88–6.88 N·m for the calves. Eight of the twelve joints sit below the 3.2 N·m threshold; the four knee joints sit above it. This is why posture dominates knee dissipation while jitter dominates the non-weight-bearing joints, and why the single most-cited piece of evidence for the jitter hypothesis is a G1 ankle-roll motor, one of the least loaded joints on a humanoid leg.

At 0.5 m/s the delayed filter reduces heat by 12% and never falls: evaluated there alone it would be recorded as the best scheme tested. Removing the effort terms moves 25 percentage points of heat into the filter-reachable band, from 18.1% to 43.3%, and inflates total dissipation by 47%. The predictive filter returns 47% and 54% of the reachable budget on the two policies. The raw saving differs by nearly a factor of three, 8.5% against 23.5%, and yet the recovered fraction is stable. This is the practically useful form of the result: measure the band split of one baseline rollout, halve the reachable share, and you have an estimate of the closed-loop saving before writing any filter code.

Recovery is not bounded by predictor accuracy. Closed-loop predictability falls from \(R^2 = 0.609\) to 0.402 between the two policies, and the wasteful policy nonetheless gives up more of its heat. On that policy, gating removes 12.1% of the heat for a 15% tracking penalty against the ungated filter's 23.5% reduction for a 59% penalty. Delayed filtering fails outright there: 37% falls overall, and every episode at 1.0 m/s. The policy that most needs help is the one the obvious intervention hurts most.

6.Discussion and Practical Guidance

Profile the spectral split before intervening. A single baseline rollout establishes the reachable budget, and halving it estimates the attainable closed-loop recovery. If the reachable share is small, command filtering cannot prevent thermal overload and the effort belongs in the reward function or the posture instead.

Do not buy zero phase with delay. Filter latency is subtracted from the policy's own stability margin, and the resulting falls appear only at speed. Filtering architectures must be evaluated at the maximum commanded velocity, not the convenient one.

Gate by load. Restrict filtering to joints operating below \(\bar{\tau} \approx \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\tau)}\). On the Go1 this preserved stability while recovering most of the tracking fidelity the ungated filter gave away.

Prefer effort regularization to action_rate. Reward formulations aimed at thermal safety should penalize joint torque and energy rather than discrete action differences, which govern 8% of the transient magnitude against 89%.

7.Related Work

Thermal-aware locomotion. Recent methods add motor temperature observations and thermal reward bounds,[2] or train residual policies conditioned on thermal state.[3] Contact-constrained inverse kinematics has addressed multi-limbed humanoid thermal redistribution.[9] All require retraining; we characterize what a post-hoc intervention on a frozen checkpoint can and cannot do.

Actuation interfaces. Related architectures include actuator reality shaping via two-degree-of-freedom tracking controllers,[10] high-frequency linear feedback for torque interpolation,[11] voltage-realizable acceleration bounding,[12] action chunk optimization,[13] B-spline policy representations,[14] and forward delay compensation networks.[15] Our contribution is the thermal-loss consequence of these interfaces rather than a new one.

Smoothness regularization. CAPS,[5] Lipschitz-constrained networks[16] and spectral normalization[17] suppress high-frequency oscillation, and a benchmark study reports improved control smoothness at small performance cost.[18] None of this work measures physical electrical dissipation or separates steady holding torque from command ripple on a legged system.

Control rate and discretization. Robust locomotion has been shown at 8 Hz on ANYmal C, with low-rate policies less sensitive to actuation latency;[19] Q-learning degenerates as the time step shrinks;[20] policies that choose their own action durations reduce average control frequency without losing reward.[21] None of it reports a thermal or electrical quantity as a function of control rate.

8.Limitations

All experiments are rigid-body simulation using mean squared torque \(\mathbb{E}[\tau^2]\) as an electrical loss proxy under constant \(K_t\); iron losses, inverter switching dissipation and winding thermal diffusion are omitted. The single-joint sweeps omit contact dynamics entirely. Everything is measured on one quadruped platform on flat terrain, so the cross-morphology claim the introduction leans on — that the G1 ankle-roll case is the same phenomenon — is argued, not demonstrated. The 5 Hz gait split is a hand-chosen parameter based on the 2 Hz stride fundamental and has not been swept. The reward ablations of Section 5.2 are single-seed over 8 s intervals, with no confidence intervals reported anywhere in this draft (pending, Appendix C). No hardware measurement supports the 3.2 N·m crossover.

9.Conclusion

Actuator overheating in learned legged locomotion decomposes into steady posture torque and dynamic command ripple. Because the two combine in quadrature, command filtering offers meaningful thermal relief only on lightly loaded joints below the crossover. In closed loop the phase lag that smoothing introduces is taken directly out of the policy's stability margin, so causal predictive filtering with load-dependent gating — not zero-phase smoothing — is what can safely recover actuator thermal margin on a frozen checkpoint.
